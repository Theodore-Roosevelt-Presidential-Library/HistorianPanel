# How T.R. scholarship has changed

Read `reference/panel-rules.md` at the plugin root first.

## Scope

- Claims about scholarly consensus or disagreement ("historians agree",
  "most scholars", "is now widely seen").
- Claims about reputation over time ("long overlooked", "once dismissed",
  "rediscovered").
- Presidential rankings and surveys of historians.
- Phases of T.R. scholarship, as scope for looking: memorial-era lives and the
  Roosevelt Memorial Association's work; Henry F. Pringle's 1931 biography and
  its reception; the edited *Letters* (Elting E. Morison and others,
  1951 onward) and the scholarship built on them (Blum, Beale, Harbaugh); the
  later popular biographies, beginning with Edmund Morris; recent work on race,
  gender, empire and Indigenous policy (for example Thomas G. Dyer, *Theodore
  Roosevelt and the Idea of Race*, 1980; Gail Bederman, *Manliness and
  Civilization*, 1995; Kristin Hoganson, *Fighting for American Manhood*,
  1998). All secondary.

## Out of scope

- What a particular biography says → `sp-biographies`
- Legends and misattributions → `sp-myths`
- The events themselves → the subject specialist (for example
  `sp-conservation`, `sp-officeholder-sequences`)
- Race, Indigenous policy and other hard chapters as history → flag for the
  Library's human reviewers via `sp-hard-chapters`
- Claims about living historians' current views → flag for the Library's
  human reviewers (panel rules section 10); point to the published work and
  never characterize a living scholar beyond it

## Where to look first

1. **Historiographical essays** in edited volumes, for example Serge Ricard,
   ed., *A Companion to Theodore Roosevelt* (2011), which surveys the
   literature. Secondary; best single map of who argued what, and when.
2. **Journal articles and review essays** on JSTOR (jstor.org), especially the
   *Journal of the Gilded Age and Progressive Era*
   (cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-gilded-age-and-progressive-era).
   Good for dated statements of where the field stood.
3. **Book reviews at publication** (H-Net Reviews; journal reviews on JSTOR;
   period newspaper reviews). They show how a book was received in its own
   time, which is what reputation claims are about.
4. **The surveys themselves** for ranking claims: the C-SPAN Presidential
   Historians Survey (c-span.org/presidentsurvey2021) and other published
   polls. Cite the specific survey and year; methods differ between polls.
5. **The prefaces and introductions** of the major works (Pringle, Morison's
   *Letters*, Morris, Dalton), where authors state what they were reacting
   against. Read them in the edition cited.
6. **Theodore Roosevelt Center digital library** (theodorerooseveltcenter.org)
   for Theodore Roosevelt Association material on memorial-era activity.
7. **Houghton Library, Theodore Roosevelt Collection at Harvard**
   (guides.library.harvard.edu/roosevelt) for the history of the collection
   that much scholarship relies on.

## Known traps

- "Historians agree" is asserted with no survey, essay or review behind it.
  Either find a published statement of consensus or soften to a named
  historian's view. [method]
- A single author's interpretation is presented as the consensus of the field.
  Credit it to the author. [method]
- A ranking from one survey is given without its year, or results from
  different surveys are combined. Name the survey and year. [method]
- A reputation shift is dated to the wrong decade, or credited to the wrong
  book. Check publication years in a catalog before writing "after X". [method]
- "Long overlooked" or "little known" is used for a subject with a substantial
  literature. Search catalogs and JSTOR before letting it stand. [method]
- Recent critical work on race and empire is presented as either settled or
  fringe. Report the range of published views and who holds them. [method]
- Present-day political framing is placed on past scholarship. Describe what
  historians argued in their own terms; keep the Library nonpartisan.
  [house-rule]
- The Library's Story Guide or website is cited as evidence of what
  historians think. It is a lead only. [house-rule]

## Procedure

1. Restate the claim as a claim about scholarship: who, when, and what view.
2. Find a historiographical essay or review essay that addresses that view.
   Note its date.
3. Identify the key works the claim depends on and confirm their publication
   years in a catalog.
4. For consensus claims, look for a published survey or a statement by more
   than one independent scholar. If you find only one voice, say so.
5. For rankings, cite the exact survey, year and category.
6. Write the narrowest accurate wording, usually crediting named historians
   rather than "historians" in general.
