# Domestic policy and executive actions

Read `reference/panel-rules.md` at the plugin root first.

## Scope

- Antitrust: suits filed under T.R., their filing dates, court decisions and
  outcomes; the Bureau of Corporations.
- Railroad and commerce regulation: the Elkins Act, the Hepburn Act, the
  Interstate Commerce Commission.
- Labor: the anthracite coal strike, the commission, and labor policy.
- Meat inspection and pure food and drug legislation.
- Civil service policy as President (his earlier Commission years go to
  `sp-civil-service-police`).
- Executive orders and proclamations that are not conservation designations:
  counts, numbering, dates signed, subject.
- Annual messages and special messages to Congress on domestic subjects.
- Cabinet departments created or reorganized, and who was appointed to them.
- "Square Deal" as a slogan: when and where he used it.

## Out of scope

- Forest reserves, monuments, bird reservations, refuges, acreage →
  `sp-conservation`
- Quote wording, speech titles → `sp-quote-verifier`
- "First," "only," "most" claims → `sp-superlatives` (also routed here)
- Order of officeholders and tenure dates → `sp-officeholder-sequences`
- Race, Indigenous policy, Brownsville, immigration restriction →
  `sp-hard-chapters`
- Indigenous land and allotment matters → flag for the Library's human
  reviewers (panel rules section 10)

## Where to look first

1. **The instrument itself.** Statutes in *U.S. Statutes at Large*
   (https://www.govinfo.gov/app/collection/statute and
   https://www.loc.gov/collections/united-states-statutes-at-large/). Gives the
   enacted text and approval date. Limit: no context, and session-law page
   citations are easy to mistype.
2. **The American Presidency Project** (https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/):
   messages to Congress, proclamations and many executive orders, with dates.
   Limit: its executive order coverage for this era is incomplete; absence there
   is not absence from the record.
3. **National Archives, executive orders**
   (https://www.archives.gov/federal-register/executive-orders) and the National
   Archives Catalog (https://catalog.archives.gov/). Explains how orders are
   numbered and where the originals are. Limit: disposition tables cover later
   presidencies far better than T.R.'s.
4. **Court records.** *United States Reports*
   (https://www.loc.gov/collections/united-states-reports/) for Supreme Court
   decisions; Oyez for case summaries. Decision dates come from the opinion, not
   from a summary.
5. **Congressional record and documents**: the Congressional Record bound
   edition (https://www.govinfo.gov/app/collection/crecb) and the Serial Set
   (https://www.govinfo.gov/app/collection/serialset) for votes, reports and the
   coal strike commission report.
6. **Agency history offices**: the FDA history pages
   (https://www.fda.gov/about-fda/fda-history) for food and drug law; the State
   Department Office of the Historian (https://history.state.gov/) for prior
   diplomatic posts of cabinet members. Good on their own agency, thin on
   politics.
7. **Period newspapers** read in the original page (Chronicling America,
   https://www.loc.gov/collections/chronicling-america/) for when a suit was
   announced versus filed. Remember a paper's issue date is not the event date.
8. **Secondary, for context only:** the Miller Center essays
   (https://millercenter.org/president/roosevelt) and standard biographies.
   Use them to find the primary record, not as the citation for a date or count.

## Known traps

- The date a suit was announced, filed, argued and decided are four different
  dates; summaries collapse them into one. Name which milestone the claim means
  and cite the court or newspaper record for it. [ref: G-08]
- An appointee's earlier post is given the wrong diplomatic rank or title
  (minister versus ambassador, commissioner versus secretary). Check the
  Office of the Historian or the official register for the title held at the
  time. [ref: E-07]
- A word or slogan is credited to T.R. as his coinage without tracing earlier
  use. Find the earliest use in a dictionary of record before saying he coined it.
  [ref: D-15]
- Counts of executive orders depend on which compilation is used and whether
  unnumbered orders, proclamations and orders later assigned numbers are
  included. Name the compilation and what it counts. [method]
- Executive orders and proclamations are treated as the same instrument, or a
  statute signed by T.R. is described as his executive action. State the
  instrument type from the document itself. [method]
- A publication series that did not yet exist is cited for an order of this
  era. Check when a series began before citing it. [method]
- The end of a strike, the operators' acceptance of a commission and the
  commission's award fall on different dates. Use the record for the milestone
  the claim names. [method]
- A law's passage, the President's signature and its effective date are
  different. Statutes at Large gives the approval date; say if the claim means
  something else. [method]
- The Library's own datasets of executive actions and its timelines are not
  sources. [house-rule]

## Procedure

1. Identify the instrument or event: statute, suit, order, proclamation,
   message, appointment, or strike settlement.
2. Pin down which milestone the claim means (announced, filed, signed,
   decided, effective, ended).
3. Find the instrument or court record itself and read the date and wording
   there.
4. For counts, name the compilation, what it includes, and do the count
   yourself; show it in `notes`.
5. Find a second independent source (a period newspaper, the Congressional
   Record, an agency history) that does not derive from the first.
6. Check the day of the week for any date.
7. Write the narrowest accurate correction, naming the milestone and the
   instrument type.
