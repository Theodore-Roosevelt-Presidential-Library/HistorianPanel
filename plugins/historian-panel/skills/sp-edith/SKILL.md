# Edith Kermit Carow Roosevelt

Read `reference/panel-rules.md` at the plugin root first.

## Scope

- Edith's family, childhood and youth in New York, including her friendship
  with the Roosevelt children and with Corinne.
- Her courtship with and marriage to T.R., the wedding and the honeymoon.
- Sagamore Hill as her household: management, money, servants, the children's
  upbringing, and her raising of her stepdaughter Alice.
- Her years as First Lady: White House entertaining, the social secretary, the
  renovation of the Executive Mansion, the china and portrait collections, and
  her part in family travel.
- Claims about her influence on T.R.'s decisions and her temperament.
- Widowhood: travels, writing, public stands, Sagamore Hill after T.R., and
  her death and burial.

## Out of scope

- Offices, ranks and careers of her children → `sp-family-offices`
- Alice's own life after childhood → `sp-alice-longworth`
- Ancestry and descent claims (for example, descent from a colonial figure) →
  `sp-generations`
- Quotations by or about Edith → `sp-quote-verifier` (also routed here for
  context)
- "First First Lady to..." and other firsts → `sp-superlatives` (also routed
  here)
- Image captions and identifications → `sp-photo-id`
- Claims about living descendants → the Library's human reviewers

## Where to look first

1. **Sagamore Hill National Historic Site (National Park Service),
   nps.gov/sahi, and the NPS people page for Edith Carow Roosevelt.** Good for
   house history, household life and the site's own collections. Interpretive
   pages are summaries; follow them to the records they cite.
2. **Theodore Roosevelt Papers, Library of Congress
   (loc.gov/collections/theodore-roosevelt-papers).** T.R.'s letters to and
   about Edith, family correspondence and diaries. Primary, but family letters
   are often undated or dated only by day of the week; date them from context.
3. **Theodore Roosevelt Collection, Houghton Library, Harvard
   (library.harvard.edu/collections/theodore-roosevelt-collection).** Holds
   much family correspondence and papers of family members. Use the finding
   aids to locate Edith's letters; many items are not digitized.
4. **Theodore Roosevelt Center digital library
   (theodorerooseveltcenter.org).** Scans and item records drawn from the
   Library of Congress, Houghton and other holders. Good for reading the
   letter itself. Its encyclopedia entries are summaries, not primary.
5. **White House Historical Association (whitehousehistory.org), including
   its First Lady biography of Edith Roosevelt.** Strong on the renovation,
   White House furnishings and social history. Secondary; check it against
   period records for dates and figures.
6. **Period newspapers, through Chronicling America
   (chroniclingamerica.loc.gov) and other holders' scans.** Society pages
   report weddings, travels and White House events. Issue dates are not event
   dates, and society reporting is often gossip.
7. **Sylvia Jukes Morris, *Edith Kermit Roosevelt: Portrait of a First Lady*
   (1980).** Secondary. The standard full biography and a good map to letters
   and diaries. Cite the documents it cites after reading them where you can.

## Known traps

- Wedding and ceremony details are embellished beyond what the record shows
  (the size of the church, the mood, who attended, the weather). Keep only
  what a record states. [method]
- Her widowhood is written as a quiet retreat when the record shows something
  else, or the reverse. Check passports, travel reporting and her own
  publications before characterizing those decades. [method]
- Her political role is framed with a superlative ("almost unprecedented for
  a woman of her era") that no source supports. Describe what she did; send
  any comparison to `sp-superlatives`. [ref: B-04]
- Spans between family events are estimated instead of computed (how long
  Alice lived apart from her father before the remarriage, how old a child was
  at a move). Compute from two sourced dates. [method]
- Characterizations of her temperament or of her relationship with Alice come
  from one later memoir and are presented as settled. Name whose view it is.
  [method]
- The White House renovation is credited to her alone or described with
  figures and dates from a secondary summary. Find the congressional
  appropriation, the architects' records or the WHHA account and say whose
  decision each part was. [method]
- Children's schooling and household arrangements in the White House years
  are assumed rather than checked. Look for the school records or period
  reporting. [method]
- Her surname is misspelled or her middle name is confused with her son
  Kermit's name. Check the spelling in a family record. [method]
- The Library's own Roosevelt family pages, family tree and timelines are not
  sources. [house-rule]

## Procedure

1. Pin the claim to a date or date range, a place and the people involved.
2. Look for a contemporary record first: a family letter or diary entry, an
   official record (marriage register, passenger list, appropriation), or a
   period newspaper read on the original page.
3. Add an independent second source: an NPS or WHHA page that cites records,
   or the Morris biography, never two summaries that share one upstream source.
4. For any span or age, compute it from the two dates and show the arithmetic.
5. For characterizations (influence, temperament, closeness), name the source
   whose judgment it is and narrow the wording to what that source supports.
6. Hand off offices of her children, quotes and firsts as listed above.
7. Write the narrowest accurate correction.
