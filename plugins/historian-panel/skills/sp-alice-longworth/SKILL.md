# Alice Roosevelt Longworth

Read `reference/panel-rules.md` at the plugin root first.

## Scope

- Alice's birth, her mother's death and her care in early childhood, and her
  place in the household after T.R. remarried.
- Her White House years: public celebrity, press coverage, debut, conduct
  that made news, and stories about her pets, cars and habits.
- The 1905 trip to Asia with the Taft party: route, people, events and her
  status on the trip.
- The 1906 White House wedding to Nicholas Longworth and the gifts, guests
  and reporting around it.
- Her Washington life: her husband's career as she figured in it, her house,
  her salon, her political alignments, and the 1912 and 1919-1920 fights as
  she took part.
- *Crowded Hours* (1933) and her other writing; interviews late in life.
- Whether a quip or line is attributed to the right speaker and occasion.

## Out of scope

- Exact wording of any quotation by her, by T.R. about her, or by others →
  `sp-quote-verifier` (also routed here for the occasion and attribution)
- Nicholas Longworth's offices, terms and House leadership →
  `sp-officeholder-sequences`
- Edith's own life → `sp-edith`; her half-siblings' careers →
  `sp-family-offices`
- The diplomacy of the 1905 Taft mission (the Taft-Katsura memorandum) →
  `sp-foreign-policy`
- "First" or "only" claims → `sp-superlatives`
- Claims about her living descendants → the Library's human reviewers

## Where to look first

1. **Alice Roosevelt Longworth, *Crowded Hours* (1933).** Her own account,
   written decades later. Primary for what she said about herself, not for
   dates or for other people's words. Label anything drawn from it as her
   recollection.
2. **Theodore Roosevelt Papers, Library of Congress
   (loc.gov/collections/theodore-roosevelt-papers), and the Theodore
   Roosevelt Center digital library (theodorerooseveltcenter.org).** Letters
   between Alice and her father and family letters about her. Read the scan
   and its item record.
3. **Library of Congress Manuscript Division finding aids
   (findingaids.loc.gov).** Locate papers of Alice and of the Longworth family
   and the Roosevelt family; finding aids describe what exists and how it is
   arranged, which helps with negative claims.
4. **Period newspapers, through Chronicling America
   (chroniclingamerica.loc.gov) and other holders' scans.** Essential for
   1902-1912 society and political reporting. Many famous stories began as
   gossip items; report what the paper said and when, not that it happened.
5. **Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art archives, "Alice in Asia: The
   1905 Taft Mission" (archive.asia.si.edu/research/archives/alice).**
   Photographs and context for the Asia trip. Check people and places against
   the catalog records.
6. **White House Historical Association (whitehousehistory.org) and the
   National Park Service people page for Alice Roosevelt Longworth.** Good
   overviews of the White House years and the wedding. Secondary.
7. **Stacy A. Cordery, *Alice: Alice Roosevelt Longworth, from White House
   Princess to Washington Power Broker* (2007).** Secondary. The fullest
   modern biography and a map to letters, diaries and press. Read what it
   cites.

## Known traps

- A famous line about running the country versus controlling Alice is quoted
  as T.R.'s own words when it survives only in a friend's later recollection.
  Label it as recalled, name who recalled it and where, and send the wording
  to `sp-quote-verifier`. [ref: C-14]
- A story that newspapers reported in a later year is moved into the White
  House years to fit the narrative. Date the story by the reporting and keep
  the year the papers give. [method]
- Her status on the 1905 trip is overstated (traveling alone, unchaperoned,
  conducting diplomacy). Check the official party and its chaperones in the
  period record before characterizing her role. [method]
- Childhood is framed as distance from her father on the strength of one
  memoir or biographer. Name the source and keep the characterization to what
  it supports. [ref: A-26]
- Who cared for her after her mother's death, and for how long, is stated as
  a round figure. Compute the span from dated records and name the household.
  [method]
- Her quips circulate in several versions with different targets and
  occasions. Find the earliest printing; if only later anthologies carry it,
  say so. [method]
- Wedding facts (the date, room, officiant, guest count, gifts) are taken
  from a modern retelling. Use the period reporting of the day itself and the
  White House record. [method]
- Late-life interviews are treated as contemporary evidence for events
  seventy years earlier. Prefer the contemporary record and note the gap.
  [method]
- The Library's own family pages and tools are not sources. [house-rule]

## Procedure

1. Pin the claim to a date or date range, a place and the people involved.
2. Decide whether the claim is an event, a characterization, or a reported
   line. Send exact wording to `sp-quote-verifier`, but still check the
   occasion and speaker here.
3. For events, find a contemporary record: a family letter, an official
   record, or a period newspaper read on the original page. Note whether the
   paper reported fact or rumor.
4. Add an independent second source (a holding institution's record, the NPS
   or WHHA page, or the Cordery biography with the document it cites).
5. If the only source is *Crowded Hours* or a late interview, label the claim
   as her recollection and narrow the wording.
6. Compute any age or span from two sourced dates and show the arithmetic.
7. Write the narrowest accurate correction.
